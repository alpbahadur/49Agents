# BUILD GUIDE FOR A NON-SLOPPY LANDING PAGE BASED ON A VIDEO BY A FAMOUS DESIGN LEAD

## Core rule: make people understand the product fast

Your landing page should answer these questions almost immediately:

1. **What is this?**
2. **Is it for me?**
3. **Does it work?**
4. **Is it credible?**

A lot of the reviewed sites failed because visitors had to work too hard to figure those out.

## 1) Be literal, not vague

They repeatedly criticized pages for being too abstract, “cute,” or jargony.

What they wanted instead:

* a clear headline
* a very literal subheadline
* direct explanation of what the product does
* direct explanation of who it is for
* direct explanation of how it is different

Their point was: if your headline is a bit marketing-y, then your subheadline should be extremely concrete.

Bad pattern:

* “AI reimagined for Excel”
* “Detailed brand videos”
* “Progressive discovery, smart navigation, precise execution”

Better pattern:

* say the actual user
* say the actual problem
* say the actual outcome

Use the words **your users actually use**, not internal company language.

## 2) Talk in the user’s language, not your own invented concepts

A big theme was that founders often describe products using terms they made up internally, but users do not think that way.

Instead:

* describe the current world the user lives in
* describe the pain/problem in familiar words
* explain the new way your product improves it

So not:

* “progressive discovery”
* “autonomous Excel agent”
* “AI-native infrastructure”

But more like:

* “Too many tools cause wrong API calls and higher costs”
* “Generate bookkeeping and tax workflows for US startups”
* “AI voice agents for inbound and outbound customer calls”

## 3) Above the fold matters a lot

They emphasized that the first screen is critical.

Above the fold should have:

* a clear message
* one main CTA
* no obvious visual bugs
* a strong first impression
* enough information to understand the product

If there are alignment issues, awkward borders, clipped video, bad hover states, confusing logos, or weird spacing right at the top, it immediately makes the site feel low quality.

## 4) Have one main CTA, not many competing ones

They kept pointing out pages with too many buttons and distractions:

* docs
* GitHub
* Discord
* pricing
* sign up
* product hunt
* YC badge
* book demo
* multiple nav actions

Their advice:

* each section should have a clear priority
* there should be **one primary CTA**
* don’t make everything shout equally

Also, don’t push money or signup too early before the visitor understands the product.

## 5) Don’t hide the explanation behind visuals

Several sites relied too much on flashy videos or demos without explaining what was actually happening.

Their view:

* visuals can support the story
* visuals should not replace the story
* if you show a demo/video, the visitor should still understand what they are looking at and why it matters

A strong landing page does not assume the product screenshots “speak for themselves.”

## 6) Show the product clearly

They liked pages more when the product itself was visible and understandable.

Good product presentation means:

* show the actual thing
* zoom into specific useful product details
* don’t use giant far-away screenshots where nobody can tell what’s happening
* use visuals that reinforce the message

If the product integrates with lots of tools, they even suggested simply showing all those tools clearly rather than hiding them behind motion or tabs.

## 7) Motion should guide attention, not steal it

They were not anti-animation. They just wanted it used carefully.

Good motion:

* helps direct attention at the right moment
* reveals things progressively
* supports the story

Bad motion:

* grabs attention too early
* moves while people are trying to read
* feels distracting
* scroll hijacks
* random loading messages/spinners that don’t help

So the rule is: motion must be functional, not decorative.

## 8) Clean up the basics before trying to be fancy

This was probably the biggest anti-“AI slop” advice.

They said to avoid the common default AI-generated style choices:

* huge shadows
* purple gradients
* bad typography
* generic icons
* sloppy styling
* random visual effects

Their recommendation was basically:

* if you are unsure, go safe
* use simple/system fonts
* keep typography clean
* get the basics right
* don’t force a fancy style you can’t control well

They explicitly said a page can be simple and plain and still be very good.

## 9) Build a strong design system / foundation

A really useful point they made:

If you have a solid foundational set of:

* design tokens
* spacing rules
* typography rules
* components

…then AI can compose those pieces fairly well.

In other words, AI output looks sloppy when the foundation is sloppy.
The fix is not “be more creative,” it is often “have better structure and constraints.”

## 10) Consistency is what makes it feel real

A lot of pages felt “vibe coded” because different parts did not feel like the same product.

Examples they criticized:

* different colors/styles across pages
* inconsistent image treatments
* inconsistent screenshot sizes
* some things with borders, others without
* hover states behaving oddly
* different visual languages on different pages
* main site and product pages feeling disconnected

A strong site should feel like one coherent system.

## 11) Details are what separate real design from AI slop

This came up again and again.

What made pages feel AI-generated:

* alignment slightly off
* broken clipping
* awkward hover behavior
* mismatched border radii
* inconsistent spacing
* different shadow treatments
* weird box-on-box-on-box layering
* random unexplained toggles
* poor section hierarchy

Their message was basically: the details are not decoration — they are the proof that the design is intentional.

## 12) Headlines matter more than subtext

They said many people scroll and only read the headlines first.

So headlines need to be:

* explicit
* meaningful on their own
* not generic
* not empty slogan language

Subheadline/body copy can add nuance, but the headline has to carry real meaning.

## 13) Social proof comes after clarity

A common mistake was putting credibility signals too early:

* YC logos
* badges
* investor references
* awards
* logos everywhere

They did not say to remove proof entirely.
They said **first explain the thing**.

Priority order:

1. what is this?
2. is it for me?
3. then credibility / proof

If your YC logo is more visually prominent than your own logo, that is a problem.

## 14) Say who it’s for clearly

Several sites improved once they clarified the target audience.

Examples:

* investment banks / consultants / analysts
* enterprise call centers
* US-based C-corps, LLCs, PBCs
* developers who want project-based practice

Visitors should not have to infer the audience from scattered clues lower on the page.

## 15) Explain how you are different

“Looks cool” is not enough.

They wanted landing pages to answer:

* Why this and not a competitor?
* What is special here?
* What can I do here that I can’t do elsewhere?

A lot of the reviewed sites failed because they showed something impressive-looking but never explained the differentiation.

## 16) Avoid naming confusion

If the product, company, open-source project, and commercial offering all have different names, it creates friction immediately.

Their advice was simple:

* pick one name when possible
* reduce confusion
* don’t make people decode your structure

## 17) Brand identity matters — but don’t force it

They noticed many sites used basically the same startup landing-page template:

* little announcement bar
* two hero buttons
* generic logo
* screenshot on the right
* YC badge somewhere
* standard SaaS layout

Their point was not that templates are always bad.
It was that if you want to be memorable, you need to go beyond the template and make the site specifically reflect:

* your product
* your audience
* your difference

But they also said simple/plain is fine. The goal is not to be flashy; it’s to feel intentional.

## 18) Section hierarchy should feel obvious

They criticized pages where section breaks and layout treatments made the structure confusing.

Good hierarchy means:

* sections feel grouped correctly
* cards belong clearly to the heading above them
* visual treatments do not accidentally imply the wrong relationship
* decorative lines/overlaps do not confuse meaning

So even a visually nice page can fail if the grouping feels off.

## 19) Remove friction and let people try it

For interactive products, they preferred:

* easy demo access
* fewer signup walls
* immediate ability to play
* constrained demos that show value fast

A landing page should reduce the work needed to understand the product.

## 20) For AI products, loading states need design too

This was more product-UX than pure landing-page advice, but very relevant.

When the user waits:

* show meaningful progress/state
* show what’s happening
* help focus attention
* avoid useless spinners and random placeholder text

Otherwise it feels cheap and confusing.

---

## Their overall formula for a good non-sloppy landing page

A great landing page should be:

* **clear**
* **specific**
* **consistent**
* **simple**
* **user-language first**
* **detail-oriented**
* **visually restrained**
* **focused on one main action**
* **honest about what the product is**
* **showing the product clearly**
* **credible, but only after clarity**

And to avoid AI slop, especially avoid:

* purple gradients everywhere
* giant shadows
* generic icons
* jargon
* too many CTAs
* inconsistent styles
* attention-grabbing motion with no purpose
* template-looking structure with no product-specific thinking
* sloppy details

## Best one-line summary of their philosophy

**Don’t try to sound smart or look fancy — make it instantly obvious what the product is, who it’s for, why it matters, and make every detail feel intentional.**
